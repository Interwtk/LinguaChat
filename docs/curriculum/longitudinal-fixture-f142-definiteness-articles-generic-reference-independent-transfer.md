## Six deterministic synthetic longitudinal histories

**A — First mention, re-mention and audience common ground.** A fictional receptionist says **“A visitor left a blue umbrella. The visitor will return at four.”** The indefinite introduces a visitor; the definite re-mentions that discourse referent. Learner A independently assumes the building has exactly one visitor; learner B identifies the previously introduced visitor without making that global claim. Counterfactual: the receptionist has mentioned two visitors and now says “the visitor” without another cue; identity may be unresolved, so B asks which visitor rather than inventing one. Another counterfactual: “the receptionist” may be identifiable from the building role without prior mention, provided that role and common ground actually support it. A colored diagram connecting “a visitor” and “the visitor” **rendered before** the response is answer-bearing assistance.

**B — Definiteness does not imply worldwide uniqueness.** In a fictional office with multiple printers, the speaker and recipient are standing beside printer P2 and hear **“Please restart the printer.”** In this shared context P2 is identifiable; the utterance does not assert that only one printer exists in the office. Learner A rejects “the” because three printers exist; learner B correctly uses the context to identify P2. Counterfactual: remove the pointing, shared location and previous reference; with three equally salient printers, the learner should ask **“Which printer?”** rather than guess. Counterfactual two: “Restart a printer” does not license arbitrarily choosing a device when a separate safety rule requires approval; task-world permissions are independent of article semantics. A visual arrow pointing at P2 before first authorship makes that trial assisted.

**C — Generic/kind reference and mass/count context.** A fictional learner reads **“I like coffee”** in a general-preference conversation and **“I like the coffee you made today”** in a tasting conversation. The first expresses a general preference for coffee; the second evaluates contextually identified coffee. Learner A always inserts “the” before an English mass noun because the Spanish prompt reads **“Me gusta el café”**; learner B independently produces the appropriate reviewed expression in each language and context. Counterfactual: a café order of **“Two coffees, please”** can refer to two servings in a reviewed English service context; this does not mean that all coffee is countable or specify serving volume. “A university” versus “an hour” requires reviewed English sound-based article selection, not first-letter spelling; do not substitute that phonological subskill for the assessed reference objective. Japanese bare-noun examples require their own independently reviewed discourse oracle rather than an invented article slot.

**D — Existence and specificity under requests or intentions.** Fictional messages contrast **“I met a translator yesterday”**, **“I need a translator for tomorrow”** and **“I need the translator we spoke to yesterday.”** The first asserts an encounter with an actual person in the described past event; the second need not establish that any particular translator currently exists or has been chosen; the third relies on a contextually identifiable prior contact. Learner A infers that a named translator has already been hired from the second sentence; learner B preserves uncertainty and asks whether a person has been selected. Counterfactual: add **“I already booked one; her name is Lea”** and the commitment changes. Another counterfactual: **“I don't need a translator”** must not be reinterpreted as proof that no translators exist. Indefinite does not imply that the speaker cannot identify the person in other contexts; specific versus non-specific readings must be judged under the reviewed discourse.

**E — Actually delivered modality, accessibility and authorship chronology.** The authoritative written instruction is **“Give the form to a supervisor, then ask the supervisor to sign it.”** An ASR transcript drops the second article, captions substitute “the” for the first “a”, a translation erases the re-mention link, a screen-reader sequence detaches the referent, or a visual diagram highlights one supervisor before the response. Record the authoritative source and what was **actually delivered**. If the delivered version no longer supports the intended reference contrast, quarantine **only that affected judgment** as UNSUPPORTED rather than mark the learner wrong. At 10:00 a learner independently asks “Which supervisor should sign?”; a referent diagram queued at 09:59 but rendered at 10:01 does not retroactively contaminate the 10:00 attempt, while the edited answer at 10:02 after exposure is ASSISTED. Autocorrect that inserts an article before submission is not unaided production. Replayed event IDs cannot create new attempts, distinct sessions or delayed gains. Accessibility alternatives must preserve the tested distinction without revealing the target referent.

**F — Longitudinal method selection, confounds and unseen audience transfer.** Fictional learner A improves immediate fill-in accuracy after FIRST_MENTION_THEN_REMENTION but repeatedly misidentifies recipients in delayed unaided handoffs. After comparable **actually delivered** AUDIENCE_IDENTIFIABILITY_DIALOGUE and LEARNER_GENERATED_CLARIFICATION trials, A independently resolves a **new** ambiguous equipment pickup with a different audience and referent set. Fictional learner B instead improves delayed written descriptions after GENERIC_VS_EPISODIC_CONTEXT_CONTRAST and WRITTEN_HANDOFF_REWRITE. Compare baseline proficiency, referent number/salience, prior mentions, countability, lexical/topic familiarity, language variety, modality, task difficulty, delay and follow-up opportunity. If one method uses one clearly pointed-to printer and another uses three equally salient people with no context, mark METHOD_REFERENT_SALIENCE_CONFOUNDED and infer **no winner**. Swapping “umbrella” for “jacket” while preserving the same two-sentence frame and referent map is **not** unseen transfer; require a different discourse configuration, audience need or communicative purpose and an independently reviewed oracle. Track eligible/offered/rendered/completed/missing delayed probes; missing is neither success nor failure. Only repeated comparable delayed first-unaided outcomes may support a **recorded subsequent change in actually delivered instruction**, with uncertainty, continued exploration and reversal when evidence contradicts it. Music, gaming or travel interests may increase some example probabilities, but neutral, adjacent and unfamiliar contexts and all CEFR obligations remain reachable.

## Nine acceptance assertions

1. **Real objective and reviewed oracle.** A–F bind real versioned CEFR-linked objectives/blueprints/prerequisites, protected skills, target language/variety/register, discourse referents/common ground, independently reviewed accepted readings, task/rubric/evaluator, accommodations, first-unaided standard, due reviews, delayed/unseen probes and shared exit gate. Never invent a CEFR article descriptor.
2. **First mention, re-mention and licensed bridging.** A links indefinite introduction to definite re-mention, distinguishes role/bridging identifiability from arbitrary guessing, and credits clarification when several referents remain equally accessible. A prior answer-bearing referent diagram contaminates independence.
3. **Contextual identifiability without global uniqueness.** B accepts “the printer” when P2 is identifiable in shared context despite multiple printers elsewhere; without disambiguating context it accepts a targeted question. It does not infer unrelated permissions from determiner choice.
4. **Generic versus episodic and cross-language boundaries.** C distinguishes reviewed English generic mass reference from a context-specific portion, protects English/Spanish/Japanese language-specific judgments and acceptable service-context count readings, and does not reduce the construct to an orthographic a/an rule.
5. **Existence, specificity and intensional/negation scope.** D distinguishes an asserted past encounter from an unfulfilled need, a previously identified referent and a negated need; it does not invent an existing hired individual or assume indefiniteness means speaker ignorance.
6. **Actual delivery, accessibility, chronology and replay.** E compares authoritative and rendered ASR/captions/TTS/translation/visual/screen-reader/autocorrect, quarantines only affected constructs, timestamps actual help exposure versus first authorship and deduplicates immutable events. Queued-but-unrendered help does not count as exposure.
7. **Comparable and reversible instruction hypotheses.** F compares feasible, accessible, actually delivered alternatives under comparable reference salience/count, countability, modality, language, prior exposure, lexical/topic burden and delayed opportunity. Confounds or insufficient follow-up mean no method winner; contrary evidence can reverse the choice.
8. **Independent delayed retention and genuinely unseen communication.** Assisted/retried or same-day repeated answers do not become independent delayed retrieval. Require a distinct eligible later learning session, first unaided authorship, a new discourse/audience configuration and independently reviewed transfer oracle. Missing follow-up is unknown, not a fabricated success/failure. Report evidence separately for listening, reading, writing, speaking and free production.
9. **Shared standards, interest diversity and unsupported boundary.** No fixture changes CEFR objectives, evaluator thresholds, mastery gates, review obligations, Pre-A1 or level availability. Interests bias but never exclusively filter examples. A passing synthetic fixture does not prove runtime implementation, actual instructional adaptation or improved human retention; those require exact-head executable evidence and longitudinal delivered-method/outcome proof.
