## Six deterministic synthetic longitudinal histories

**A — Agent, theme, source and recipient cannot silently reverse.** A fictional logistics note says **“Nina sent the keys to Omar”**. The event oracle identifies Nina as sender, the keys as theme and Omar as intended recipient; it does **not** claim Omar received them. Learner A independently reports **“Omar sent Nina the keys”**, reversing the event; learner B reports **“Nina sent Omar the keys, but delivery is not confirmed.”** Counterfactual: **“Omar received the keys from Nina”** does assert receipt in the described event, while **“Nina received the keys from Omar”** reverses source and recipient. Counterfactual two: in **“The keys were sent to Omar by Nina”**, the grammatical subject is the keys but the agent remains Nina. A role diagram showing arrows from Nina to Omar **rendered before** the first response makes a matching answer assisted.

**B — Licensed frames, alternations and target-language boundaries.** In a reviewed standard-English handoff, **“Lea gave Max the schedule”** and **“Lea gave the schedule to Max”** express a comparable giving event, but **“Lea explained Max the schedule”** is not the corresponding licensed standard-English frame: **“Lea explained the schedule to Max”** or a separately reviewed acceptable paraphrase is required. Learner A overgeneralizes the give-frame to every verb; learner B independently selects an appropriate frame and preserves the event roles. Counterfactual: **“Lea told Max the schedule changed”** is a different licensed construction and proposition, not proof that **explain** shares its frame. A separately reviewed Spanish task contrasts **“Le di el horario a Max”** with acceptable regional clitic-doubling or pronoun choices; the evaluator must not impose English double-object order or penalize reviewed target-variety alternatives. Japanese or other language examples require independent review of case marking, omitted arguments and recipient interpretation; do not invent universal equivalents. An isolated frame-label quiz does not establish communicative mastery.

**C — Embedded actor versus main-clause actor.** A fictional coordinator reports **“Maya promised Luis to call the supplier”** and, in a second separately reviewed English context, **“Maya persuaded Luis to call the supplier.”** Under the reviewed ordinary readings, Maya is the intended caller in the promise, whereas Luis is the intended caller in the persuasion. Learner A assigns the embedded action to the nearest name in both cases; learner B independently identifies the intended actor and writes a recipient-safe task assignment. Counterfactual: **“Maya asked Luis to call the supplier”** ordinarily assigns the requested call to Luis, but does not establish that Luis agreed or called. If pronouns, ellipsis or target-variety interpretation leave the intended actor genuinely ambiguous, credit an appropriate clarification instead of forcing a fabricated assignment. Do not treat a request, promise or persuasion as evidence that the embedded action was completed.

**D — A changed verb may change the event, not merely its grammar.** A fictional inventory update says **“Ravi sent a replacement screen to the lab”**; learner A reports **“The lab received the screen”** without evidence, while learner B says **“It was sent; receipt has not been confirmed.”** Counterfactual: **“Ravi delivered the screen to the lab and obtained a signed receipt”** supplies a stronger completion claim. In a separate reviewed task, **“Mina poured water into the glass”** does not entail that the glass became full, whereas **“Mina filled the glass with water”** describes a completed filling under its ordinary reviewed reading. Learner B preserves that distinction in an independent recipient-facing report. Likewise, **showing** a book to someone does not entail **giving** it to them. Do not reward a superficially fluent paraphrase that upgrades intention to completion, changes possession, or invents a beneficiary.

**E — Actually delivered cue loss, accessibility, authorship and replay.** The authoritative task says **“Send the form to Ana, not from Ana.”** An ASR transcript drops **to**, captions replace **to** with **from**, a translation swaps source and recipient, a screen reader presents a detached arrow without its labels, or autocomplete inserts the correct preposition before submission. Record the **actually delivered** stimulus and assistance. If the delivered version no longer distinguishes source and goal, quarantine **only that affected role judgment** as UNSUPPORTED rather than mark the learner wrong. At 10:00 the learner independently asks **“Is Ana receiving or sending it?”**; a role diagram queued at 09:59 but first rendered at 10:01 does not contaminate that 10:00 answer. A revision at 10:02 after the diagram is ASSISTED. An accessible textual role description must preserve the assessed contrast without leaking the answer before the first-unaided probe. Replayed event IDs cannot create new first attempts, independent sessions or delayed successes. Unknown hearing or missing exposure timestamps remain unknown.

**F — Longitudinal adaptation, confounds and unseen role-correct handoff.** Fictional learner A improves immediately after VERB_FRAME_CONTRAST but repeatedly reverses recipients on delayed unaided handoffs. After comparable **actually delivered** ROLE_FIRST_EVENT_RECONSTRUCTION and TEXT_TO_SPOKEN_RECIPIENT_CHECK trials, A independently handles a **new** three-party equipment return with a different verb sense, recipient and audience. Learner B instead improves delayed unaided messages after ACTIVE_PASSIVE_ROLE_ALIGNMENT and LEARNER_GENERATED_FREE_HANDOFF. Compare baseline role mapping, verb frequency, number of actors, animacy, syntactic voice, lexical familiarity, prior exposure, target variety, modality, topic, review delay and actual follow-up opportunity. If one method uses familiar **give** with two animate people and another uses unfamiliar **entrust** with three roles, flag METHOD_VERB_FREQUENCY_CONFOUNDED and METHOD_ROLE_COUNT_CONFOUNDED: infer **no winner**. Replacing Nina with Sara in the same sent-keys frame and same recipient task is **not** unseen transfer; require a genuinely different event graph, communicative purpose or audience and independently reviewed oracle. Track delayed probes as eligible/offered/rendered/completed/missing; missing is neither success nor failure. Only repeated comparable delayed **first-unaided** outcomes can support a recorded subsequent change in **actually delivered** instruction, with uncertainty, alternative exploration and reversal when contradictory outcomes appear. Interests in music, travel or games may bias example frequency but cannot exclude adjacent, neutral and unfamiliar event contexts or any required CEFR objective.

## Nine acceptance assertions

1. **Real objective and independently reviewed event oracle.** A–F resolve real versioned CEFR-linked objectives/blueprints/prerequisites, protected skill obligations, target language/variety/register, independently reviewed predicate senses/argument structures/role mappings and accepted paraphrases, task/rubric/evaluator, accessibility, first-unaided threshold, due reviews, delayed/unseen probes and shared exit gate. Never invent a CEFR valency descriptor.
2. **Semantic role preservation across syntax and voice.** A distinguishes agent, theme, source and goal in active and passive constructions; swapping names or grammatical subjects must not silently swap event roles, and sending must not imply successful receipt.
3. **Licensed argument frames without overgeneralization.** B accepts reviewed give-alternations, rejects the unlicensed explain double-object frame in the specified standard-English register, credits equivalent communicative paraphrases and applies separately reviewed Spanish/Japanese or other language-specific grammar rather than projecting English word order.
4. **Embedded actor and commitment scope.** C correctly assigns who is supposed to call under reviewed promise/persuade/ask constructions, preserves uncertainty where pronouns or context are ambiguous, and never upgrades an intention or request to a completed action.
5. **Verb-sense and result entailments.** D separates send from confirmed delivery, pour from fill and show from give; an answer that changes possession, completion or beneficiary fails the affected communicative claim even if the surface grammar is fluent.
6. **Actual delivery, accessible equivalence and replay safety.** E compares authoritative versus actually delivered ASR/captions/translation/TTS/visual/screen-reader/autocomplete, quarantines only unsupported judgments, timestamps first authorship versus actual help exposure and deduplicates immutable session-qualified events. Queued-but-unrendered help does not count as exposure.
7. **Comparable, uncertain and reversible instruction.** F compares feasible and actually delivered methods under matched objective, verb frequency/sense, role count, animacy, modality, prior help, topic and follow-up. Confounding, sparse data or contradictory results yield no winner and balanced fallback; preserve bounded exploration and reversibility.
8. **Independent delayed retention, unseen transfer and balanced coverage.** Same-frame name substitutions, copied role diagrams, assisted repairs and same-session repetition cannot become independent delayed/unseen mastery. Require distinct eligible later sessions, first-unaided production, a genuinely new event graph/audience and reviewed oracle. Record missingness and skill-family evidence separately; keep all due reviews, shared thresholds and nonexclusive topic rotation.
9. **Evidence honesty and release boundary.** A passing document or CI check is **not** proof of executable engine support, observed outcome-driven changes in delivered instruction or improved human retention. Such claims require exact-head executable tests tied to real objectives, observed longitudinal method choices and independent delayed/transfer comparisons against a balanced baseline, followed by ethically reviewed learner evidence.
